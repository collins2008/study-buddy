### 1. Classification: The Three Families of Entrepreneurship Theories (Slide 20)
1. **Economic Theories**:
   - Focus on market mechanisms, resource allocation, risk-bearing, arbitrage, and technological innovation.
   - Key Thinkers: Joseph Schumpeter, Frank Knight, Israel Kirzner, Harvey Leibenstein, Peter Drucker.
2. **Psychological Theories**:
   - Focus on internal cognitive traits, personality structures, individual motives, and behavioral drives.
   - Key Thinker: David McClelland (Need for Achievement - $n\text{-Ach}$), Julian Rotter (Locus of Control).
3. **Sociological & Cultural Theories**:
   - Focus on social context, religious values, cultural heritage, family traditions, status deprivation, and community networks.
   - Key Thinkers: Max Weber (Religious values/Protestant ethic), Everett Hagen (Status withdrawal / Relative deprivation).
4. **Action & Resource Theories**:
   - Focus on internal organizational capabilities and practical cognitive decision logic.
   - Key Frameworks: Resource-Based View (RBV - Barney), Effectuation (Saras Sarasvathy).

### 2. Economic Theories
- **Joseph A. Schumpeter (Innovation & Creative Destruction)**:
  - *Premise*: Capitalism is inherently dynamic, not static. The entrepreneur breaks the circular flow of the economy by introducing **"Five New Combinations"**:
    1. Introduction of a new good or new quality of a good.
    2. Introduction of a new method of production.
    3. Opening of a new market.
    4. Conquest of a new source of supply of raw materials or half-manufactured goods.
    5. Carrying out of the new organization of any industry (e.g., breaking a monopoly).
  - *Creative Destruction*: Industrial mutation that incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within, destroying the old one and creating a new one (e.g., digital mobile banking replacing physical branches).
- **Frank H. Knight (Risk vs Uncertainty - 1921)**:
  - *Measurable Risk*: Situations where the distribution of potential outcomes is known mathematically or historically (e.g., fire, shipwreck, theft). **Risk is insurable and plannable**.
  - *True Uncertainty*: Situations where outcomes, odds, and future states are unique and fundamentally unmeasurable (e.g., launching an untried restaurant or revolutionary consumer gadget). **Uncertainty is non-insurable**.
  - *Core Insight*: Pure economic profit is the entrepreneur's reward for exercising superior judgment and bearing non-insurable uncertainty.
- **Israel Kirzner (Austrian School - Opportunity Alertness & Arbitrage)**:
  - *Premise*: Markets are rarely in equilibrium; they are plagued by ignorance and friction.
  - *Entrepreneurial Alertness*: The spontaneous, antennae-like ability to notice price discrepancies, unmet customer needs, and unperceived market gaps without incurring search costs.
  - *Role*: The Kirznerian entrepreneur does not necessarily invent or disrupt; they act as an **arbitrageur**, buying where prices are low and selling where demand is high, steadily steering the market toward equilibrium.
- **Harvey Leibenstein (X-Inefficiency & Gap-Filling)**:
  - *Premise*: Firms do not operate at peak neoclassical efficiency due to internal frictions, organizational slack, and misallocated resources (**X-Inefficiency**).
  - *Role*: The entrepreneur acts as an **input-completer** and **gap-filler**, mobilizing fragmented resources, coordinating disjointed suppliers, and overcoming market bottlenecks to restore productive efficiency.
- **Peter Drucker (Systematic Innovation)**:
  - *Premise*: Entrepreneurship is a purposeful discipline based on monitoring 7 sources of innovation (Unexpected occurrences, Incongruities, Process needs, Industry/market shifts, Demographics, Perception shifts, New knowledge).

### 3. Psychological Theory: David McClelland (Need for Achievement)
- **Acquired Needs Theory (1961)**:
  - Individuals possess varying levels of three fundamental needs: Need for Achievement ($n\text{-Ach}$), Need for Affiliation ($n\text{-Aff}$), and Need for Power ($n\text{-Power}$).
  - **High $n\text{-Ach}$** is the defining psychological driver of entrepreneurs:
    - Desire for personal responsibility and ownership of outcomes.
    - Preference for setting challenging, moderate, but achievable goals (avoiding both trivial tasks and wild long-shot gambles).
    - Insatiable demand for concrete, unambiguous, immediate feedback on performance (e.g., sales figures, customer reviews).
    - Preference for independent problem-solving and continuous performance improvement.

### 4. Sociological Theories: Weber & Hagen
- **Max Weber (Culture, Religion & Capital Accumulation)**:
  - Linked the emergence of modern capitalism and entrepreneurship to the ascetic values of the Calvinist / Protestant Ethic: hard work as a moral duty ("calling"), frugality, rational organization, and reinvestment of profits rather than hedonistic personal consumption.
  - Generalization: Cultural norms, social status expectations, and societal attitudes toward wealth accumulation, family solidarity, and business failure determine a society's entrepreneurial supply.
- **Everett Hagen (Theory of Social Change / Status Withdrawal)**:
  - Entrepreneurship emerges when a specific social or ethnic minority group experiences a historical **loss of social status** or prestige (status withdrawal/displacement).
  - The aggrieved group channels its frustrated energy into commercial trade, technical innovation, and independent enterprise as an alternative vehicle to regain respect and societal prestige (e.g., historical enterprise among minority trading castes).

### 5. Action & Resource Frameworks
- **Resource-Based View (RBV - Jay Barney)**:
  - Sustained competitive advantage derives from controlling bundles of resources and capabilities that are **VRIO**: Valuable, Rare, Inimitable (hard to copy), and Organized to capture value.
  - Resource categories: Physical/financial capital, human skills/expertise, proprietary technology, social networks, and brand reputation.
- **Saras Sarasvathy: Effectuation (Non-Predictive Control)**:
  - *Causation (Managerial logic)*: Starts with a predefined goal, then analyzes the environment and forecasts resources needed to achieve that goal.
  - *Effectuation (Entrepreneurial logic)*: Starts with existing means (**Who am I? What do I know? Whom do I know?**), then imagines possible effects that can be created, acting through 5 core principles:
    1. **Bird in Hand**: Start with your current means and capabilities; do not wait for ideal conditions or massive external funding.
    2. **Affordable Loss**: Commit only what you can afford to lose at each step; do not gamble on calculated expected return.
    3. **Crazy Quilt**: Form self-selecting, cooperative stakeholder partnerships and pre-commitments rather than conducting competitive analysis.
    4. **Lemonade**: Embrace surprises, contingencies, and negative feedback as new opportunities ("when life hands you lemons, make lemonade").
    5. **Pilot in the Plane**: Focus on the controllable aspects of the environment; the future is not predicted, it is made.
