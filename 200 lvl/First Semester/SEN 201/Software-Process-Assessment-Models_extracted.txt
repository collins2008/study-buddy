Software Process Assessment Models

A Software Process Assessment is a structured evaluation of how an organization
develops software, not just what it builds, but how it builds it.

The goal is to answer three questions:

i. Where are we now? — What is our current process capability / maturity?
ii. Where do we want to be? — What level does our business need?
iii. How do we get there? — What improvements will close the gap?

Assessment models provide the framework, questionnaire,
improvement road map to do this systematically, instead of relying on opinion.

1. Why Assess the Process?
i. Predictability: Mature processes produce predictable cost, schedule, and quality.
ii. Visibility: Management can see risks early, not at delivery time.
iii. Improvement: You cannot improve what you have not measured.
iv. Benchmarking: Compare against industry best practice and competitors.
v. Contractual confidence: Clients, especially in government, defence, banking, and

telecoms, often require a certified maturity level before awarding work.

Assessment is diagnostic, not punitive. The output is strengths, weaknesses, and an
improvement plan.

2. Core Concepts

Process Capability vs. Process Maturity
i. Capability: How well a single process area (e.g., Requirements Management,

Testing) is performed. Measured on a 0–5 scale.

ii. Maturity: How well the whole organization performs across all process areas.

Measured as a Maturity Level 1–5.

Staged vs. Continuous Representation
i. Staged: Organization gets ONE overall maturity level. You must satisfy all
practices at that level to move up. Simpler to communicate: “We are Level 3.”
ii. Continuous: Each process area gets its OWN capability level. More flexible:

You can be Level 4 in Testing but Level 2 in Configuration Management.

Appraisal Methods
i. Self-assessment: Internal team uses questionnaires. Fast, cheap, less objective.
ii. Formal appraisal: Trained, certified assessors interview staff, review documents,

and rate evidence. Expensive, but credible and auditable.

3.2 ISO/IEC 15504: SPICE (Software Process Improvement and Capability
dEtermination)
Now superseded by ISO/IEC 330xx family.

SPICE is an international standard for process assessment, not a proprietary model
like CMMI.

Key Features
i. Defines a Process Reference Model (what processes exist) and a Process

Assessment Model (how to rate them).

ii. Uses 6 Capability Levels: 0 Incomplete, 1 Performed, 2 Managed, 3 Established,

4 Predictable, 5 Optimizing.

iii. Each process is rated on Process Attributes (e.g., Process Performance, Work

Product Management, Process Definition).

iv. Rating scale: N (Not achieved 0–15%), P (Partially 16–50%), L (Largely 51–

85%), F (Fully 86–100%).

v. Strengths: International standard, flexible, can assess any process, good for

automotive (Automotive SPICE / A-SPICE is a major variant).

vi. Limitations: Less prescriptive than CMMI — tells you what to assess, not

exactly how to improve.

3.3 ISO/IEC 330xx Series
The modern replacement for 15504. It separates:

i. ISO/IEC 33001: Concepts and terminology
ii. ISO/IEC 33002: Requirements for performing process assessment
iii. ISO/IEC 33003: Requirements for process measurement frameworks
iv. ISO/IEC 33004: Requirements for process reference, assessment, and maturity

models

v. ISO/IEC 33020: Process measurement framework

Used heavily in automotive, medical devices, and safety-critical systems.

3.5 Other Notable Models
i. BOOTSTRAP: Early European model, precursor to SPICE. Focused on small-

to-medium enterprises.

ii. Trillium: Developed by Bell Canada for telecom software. Emphasizes product

quality and customer focus.

iii. ISO 9001: Not software-specific, but a Quality Management System standard.
Assesses whether you follow your documented process, not whether the process
itself is good for software.

iv. People Capability Maturity Model (P-CMM): Assesses workforce practices:

staffing, training, compensation, team building. Complements CMMI.

6. Benefits vs. Challenges

Benefits
i. Reduces defects, rework, and project overruns.
ii. Improves estimation accuracy and delivery predictability.
iii. Creates a common language for process across teams.
iv. Supports continuous improvement culture.

Challenges
i. Can become bureaucratic if implemented for certification only.
ii. High cost in time, training, and appraisal.
iii. Resistance from teams who see it as paperwork.
iv. A high maturity level does NOT guarantee a good product — it guarantees a

repeatable process.

7. Choosing a Model
Ask:

i. Who is asking? Client contract → CMMI or A-SPICE. Internal improvement →

Agile model or ISO 330xx.

ii. Organization size? Small team → Lightweight self-assessment. Large enterprise

→ CMMI.

iii. Development method? Waterfall / V-model → CMMI / SPICE. Agile / DevOps

→ Agile maturity model.

iv. Goal? Certification vs. genuine improvement - choose accordingly.

Software Process Metrics
Software Process Metrics are quantitative measures used to assess, monitor, control,
and improve the software development process itself - not just the final product.

If a product metric asks “Is this software good?”, a process metric asks “Is the way
we build software good and getting better?”

Without process metrics, improvement is based on opinion, memory, and anecdote
(short account). With them, it is based on evidence.

1. What Are Software Process Metrics?
A software process metric is a measurable attribute of the activities, resources,
methods, and tools used to develop software.

i. Process metrics measure the process (e.g., review effectiveness, defect removal

efficiency, cycle time).

ii. Product metrics measure the product (e.g., size, complexity, reliability, defect

density in released code).

iii. Project metrics measure the project

(e.g., cost, schedule, effort,

resource

utilisation).

In practice the three are linked: a weak process produces a weak product, which
causes project overruns.

Example: If peer reviews catch only 30% of defects, the process is weak. The product
will ship with more defects, and the project will spend more time on rework.

2. Why Do We Need Process Metrics?
i. Visibility: Make the process visible - expose bottlenecks, rework, waiting time,

and waste that are otherwise invisible.

ii. Objective Decisions: Provide an objective basis for decisions instead of gut

feeling.

iii. Prediction: Enable prediction - estimate future cost, schedule, and quality from

past performance.

iv. Improvement: Support continuous improvement - establish a baseline, try a

change, and measure whether it helped.

v. Maturity & Compliance: Support quality assurance and process maturity -
required for CMMI Level 4 (Quantitatively Managed) and ISO/IEC 330xx
assessments.

vi. Early Warning: Enable early warning — detect slipping quality or productivity

before delivery fails.

3. Characteristics of a Good Metric
A useful process metric must be:

i. Simple: Simple and easy to collect - if it takes too long to measure, people will

fake it or stop.

ii. Well-defined: Clearly defined - everyone must calculate it the same way.
iii. Objective: Objective - not dependent on who is measuring.
iv. Timely: Timely - available when the decision must be made, not months later.
v. Actionable: Actionable - it should suggest what to do next.
vi. Resistant to gaming: Not easy to game - if developers are rewarded only for

“lines of code”, they will write bloated code.

Golden rule: Measure to improve, not to blame. If metrics are used to punish teams,
data quality collapses.

5. Key Software Process Metrics Explained in Detail

5.1 Productivity Metrics
Measure how much useful output is produced per unit of effort.

i. Formulas / Examples: Function Points per person-month; Story Points per sprint;

Features delivered per month.

ii. LOC: Lines of Code (LOC) per person-month — simple but misleading, because

shorter, better code looks less productive.

Use with care: Productivity rises when rework falls, automation improves, and
requirements are stable — not when people simply work longer hours.

5.2 Defect-Related Metrics (Most Important)
i. Defect Removal Efficiency (DRE): Total defects found in a phase ÷ Total
defects found in that phase + defects that escaped to later phases × 100. A DRE of
85%+ for reviews is considered good.

ii. Defect Density: Defects ÷ Size (KLOC (Thousands of Lines of Code) or
Function Points). Defects found in testing + defects found after release. Higher
means poor early detection.

iii. Escaped Defects / Defect Leakage: Defects found by customers after release ÷

Total defects. Low is good. High means testing and reviews are weak.

iv. Phase Containment: Percentage of defects found in reviews vs. testing vs. field.

Ideally, most defects are found early and cheaply.

v. Phase Containment (duplicate for emphasis in teaching): Percentage of
defects found in reviews vs. testing vs. field. Ideally, most defects are found early
and cheaply.

The cost of fixing a defect grows 10x–100x from requirements to field. Process
metrics prove why early detection matters.

5.3 Time / Cycle Metrics
i. Cycle Time / Lead Time: Time from start of work on a feature / requirement to

its delivery. Shorter, stable cycle time = healthier flow.

ii. Mean Time to Repair (MTTR): Time taken to fix a defect from report to

verified fix.

iii. Schedule Adherence: Percentage of milestones / sprints delivered on time.
iv. Wait Time / Queue Time: Time spent waiting for approvals, reviews, builds, or

other teams. Often the largest hidden waste.

5.4 Effort & Cost Metrics
i. Effort Variance: (Actual Effort − Planned Effort) ÷ Planned Effort × 100.
ii. Cost Variance: (Actual Cost − Planned Cost) ÷ Planned Cost × 100.
iii. Rework Ratio: Effort spent fixing defects, redoing work, or clarifying unclear
requirements ÷ Total effort. Rework above 30–40% signals a broken upstream
process.

5.5 Review & Inspection Metrics
i. Review Coverage: Number of work products reviewed ÷ Total work products ×

100.

ii. Review Effectiveness: Defects found per review hour.

iii. Inspection Rate / Defect Density in Reviews: Defects found per 1,000 LOC or

per page inspected.

iv. Review Effort Ratio: Review effort ÷ Total development effort. Typically 10–

15% is healthy; too low means reviews are skipped.

5.6 Testing Process Metrics
i. Test Coverage: (Test cases executed ÷ Total test cases) × 100, and code / branch

coverage.

ii. Defect Detection Rate: Defects found in testing ÷ Testing effort.
iii. Test Pass Rate: Test cases passed ÷ Test cases executed × 100.
iv. Test Automation Coverage: Percentage of tests automated. Higher automation

usually means faster feedback and lower regression cost.

5.7 Change & Stability Metrics
i. Requirements Volatility: Number of requirement changes after baseline ÷ Total

requirements × 100. High volatility predicts schedule slippage.

ii. Code Churn: Lines / files changed after first release of a component. High churn

signals unstable design or unclear requirements.

iii. Build Failure Rate: Number of builds that fail ÷ Total builds. Frequent failures

signal integration or quality problems.

7. How to Implement Process Metrics - Step by Step
i. 1. Define Goals: Pick 2–3 business goals (e.g., faster delivery, fewer escaped

defects).

ii. 2. Select Metrics: Use GQM to derive 5–10 metrics, not 50.
iii. 3. Define & Standardise: Define exactly how each metric is calculated, who

collects it, and how often.

iv. 4. Collect Baseline Data: Collect for 2–3 months to establish a baseline before

judging performance.

v. 5. Analyse & Visualise: Plot trends on control charts / dashboards. Look for

trends, not single points.

vi. 6. Act & Improve: Hold retrospectives. Ask “What is the process telling us?”
vii. 7. Review & Refine: Review metrics quarterly. Drop useless metrics, add new

ones as goals change.

8. Common Pitfalls
i. Metric Overload: Collecting too many metrics - teams drown in data and act on

none of it.

ii. Blame Culture: Using metrics to punish - leads to gaming, hiding defects, and

inflated estimates.

iii. Comparing Unlike Teams: Comparing teams unfairly - different domains, sizes,

and complexities cannot be compared directly.

iv. Vanity Metrics: Focusing on vanity metrics - e.g., number of tests, without

checking if they catch defects.

v. Ignoring Context: Ignoring context - a rise in defects may mean better testing,

not worse code.

vi. One-off Measurement: Measuring once and forgetting - metrics only help if

reviewed regularly.
