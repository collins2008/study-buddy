=== SLIDE 5 ===
Entrepreneurship vs. intrapreneurship
Both involve initiative and value creation; the setting differs
5
ENTREPRENEURSHIP
• Creates or develops a venture
• Usually mobilizes resources for a new business
• Makes decisions about the offer, customers and operations
• Carries substantial responsibility for the venture

Example: a graduate launches a laundry pick-up service for students.
INTRAPRENEURSHIP
• Innovates within an existing organization
• Uses organizational resources and systems
• Develops a new product, process or service
• Works within the organization's goals and constraints

Example: a bank employee proposes a simpler digital onboarding process.
Quick check: Is the person building a venture, or innovating inside an organization?

=== SLIDE 6 ===
2. Why do people become entrepreneurs?
Two common starting points: necessity and opportunity
6
NECESSITY-BASED
A person starts an activity because other income or employment options are limited.

Example: someone begins selling snacks to support household expenses.
OPPORTUNITY-BASED
A person sees a promising unmet need and chooses to pursue it.

Example: a caterer notices demand for pre-ordered office lunches and creates a subscription service.
Important distinction
These are useful lenses, not fixed labels. A necessity-started business can become innovative and growth-oriented; an opportunity-led venture can still face severe constraints.

=== SLIDE 11 ===
Theory spotlight: Joseph Schumpeter
Entrepreneurs drive change by introducing new combinations
7
INNOVATION
NEW PRODUCT
A different or improved offer
NEW PROCESS
A faster or cheaper method
NEW MARKET
Serve a new group or location
NEW BUSINESS MODEL
Change how value is delivered or paid for
Creative destruction: new ways of doing things can replace older products, services or business models.

=== SLIDE 12 ===
Creative destruction: a familiar example
Innovation can create value while challenging established businesses
8
EARLIER PATTERN
Customers visit several shops or call multiple sellers to compare availability and prices.

Information is scattered; transactions take time.
NEW
MODEL
DIGITAL MARKETPLACE
A platform can help buyers compare offers, contact sellers and coordinate transactions.

Convenience improves, but some traditional intermediaries must adapt.
Nigerian application: online food ordering, digital payments and e-commerce have changed how some customers discover, pay for and receive goods. The impact varies by sector and location.
Ask: Who gains value? Who faces pressure to change? What new risks emerge?

=== SLIDE 14 ===
Mini case: a campus laundry service
Apply the concepts—do not assume the idea is automatically viable
10
PROBLEM
Students have limited time and inconsistent access to reliable laundry.
OPPORTUNITY
Offer scheduled pick-up and delivery with clear prices.
TEST
Survey 30 students; pilot with 10 customers.
MEASURE
Track costs, turnaround, repeat orders and complaints.
Student task: identify the assumptions that must be tested.
1
Will students pay the proposed price?
2
Can collection and delivery be reliable?
3
What are the costs per order and expected margin?
4
How will clothes be labelled and losses handled?
5
What would make customers return?

=== SLIDE 15 ===
Class activity: opportunity hunt
Work in pairs or small groups • 8–10 minutes
11
Choose one real problem you have observed on campus or in your community.
1. PROBLEM
Who experiences it, and how often?
2. CUSTOMER
Who would use or pay for a solution?
3. SOLUTION
What is your simplest useful offer?
4. EVIDENCE
How can you test demand this week?
Prepare a 60-second pitch using this sentence:
PITCH TEMPLATE
“We noticed that [customer] struggles with [problem]. We propose [solution]. We will test it by [small experiment] and measure success using [evidence].”
