Why entrepreneurship matters
Economic development
Entrepreneurs turn opportunities into businesses.
Innovation

Wealth generation

Scholars disagree on why people become entrepreneurs, hence several theories.

=== SLIDE 6 ===
Schumpeter: innovation theory
New product or service
New production method
New market
New raw-material source
New form of organisation
Entrepreneurs are agents of change who combine resources in new ways.
Relevance
Explains why innovation and adaptation matter for survival and growth.
6
=== SLIDE 7 ===
Schumpeter: creative destruction
New technology or model
Older products replaced
Weak firms lose customers
Productivity improves
Example: digital banking
Mobile payments changed how people pay and transfer money. Traditional businesses that fail to adapt may lose customers to more innovative rivals.

=== SLIDE 8 ===
Knight: risk and uncertainty
Risk
Probabilities can be estimated
Insurable or plannable
Uncertainty
Outcomes and odds cannot be known
Entrepreneurs bear the consequences
Restaurant: demand, rivals and costs are unknown, so the profit or loss follows the judgement.

=== SLIDE 9 ===
McClelland: need for achievement
Challenging goals
Difficult but attainable targets.
Personal responsibility
Owns the results.
Seeks feedback
Wants to know performance.
Solves problems alone
Prefers independent effort.
Keeps improving
Looks for better ways.
Example
A fashion owner sets sales targets and studies customer feedback.

=== SLIDE 10 ===
Weber: culture and values
Work and wealth
Attitudes shape effort.
Family traditions
Skills and contacts pass on.
Community networks
Support and trust.
Attitude to failure
Affects risk taking.
Social status
Expectations of success.
Saving and investment
Values of conduct.
Weber linked religious and cultural orientations to attitudes towards work and saving.

=== SLIDE 11 ===
Hagen: status withdrawal
Loss of status
Dissatisfaction
New skills, independence
New venture
Limit
Not everyone who loses status becomes an entrepreneur: finance, skills and networks also matter. Example: a retrenched professional starts a consultancy.

=== SLIDE 12 ===
Leibenstein: X-efficiency
Market gap or waste
Organise fragmented resources
Better delivery
Higher productivity
Example
Small retailers cannot get affordable delivery. An entrepreneur coordinates riders, shops and digital ordering to cut the inefficiency.

Kirzner: opportunity alertness
Unmet needs
Gaps in what customers get.
Price differences
Across markets.
Changing behaviour
Shifting preferences.
Underserved groups
Locations or customers.
Connecting buyers and sellers
More efficiently.
Example
Students lack late meals, so a delivery service fills the gap.

Financial capital
Funds to invest.
Skills and knowledge
Human expertise.
Technology
Equipment and systems.
Networks
Relationships.

Trust of customers.
Processes

=== SLIDE 16 ===
Effectuation: start with what you have
Who am I?
What do I know?
Whom do I know?
What can I do now?
Then work with partners, test ideas and adapt as circumstances change.
Contrast
Causal approaches fix a goal first, then calculate the resources needed.

=== SLIDE 17 ===
Effectuation: five principles
Bird in hand
Start with available means.
Affordable loss
Risk only what you can lose.
Crazy quilt
Build partnerships.
Lemonade
Use surprises well.
Pilot in the plane
Focus on what you can influence.
Example
A graduate sells cakes to friends, reinvests, then opens a bakery.

=== SLIDE 20 ===
Three families of explanation
Economic
Schumpeter, Knight, Kirzner, Leibenstein, Drucker: innovation, risk, opportunity.
Psychological
McClelland: motives, goals and persistence.
Sociological
Weber, Hagen: culture, family, status and networks.
Each is partial: a full picture combines all three.

Applying the theories in Nigeria
Fintech and digital services
Schumpeter: payments reshape finance.
Agriculture and food

Fashion and creative work

Illustrations, not proof that one theory explains everything.
